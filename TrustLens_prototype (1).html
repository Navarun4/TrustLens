What TrustLens did not check

  • Who actually sent the message — sender names and numbers can be faked.
  • Where any link really leads, its owner, age, certificate or reputation.
  • Whether you have an account, order or case with the organisation named.
  • Scam styles outside its fixed pattern list, or languages other than English (and a few romanised Hindi words).
Educational tool, not a verified fraud detector. Detected signals are patterns in the text, not confirmed facts about the sender. A low score does not mean a message is safe — confirm independently through an official app, website or a phone number you already trust.

How TrustLens works

Plain explanation of the rules, the points and the limits.

1. Normalise the text

The message is converted to a standard Unicode form (so “ＯＴＰ” in full-width letters reads as “OTP”) and invisible characters such as zero-width spaces are removed. Their presence — and words that mix Latin with Cyrillic or Greek look-alike letters — are reported as disguise signals. Only the first 10,000 characters are analysed.

2. Match a fixed list of patterns

Each rule is a hand-written pattern with a weight. Rules belong to categories so that related evidence isn't counted twice:

CategoryRuleWeight

3. Combine points without double-counting

  • Overlap discount: if all the words behind a signal were already used as evidence by a stronger signal, it counts at half weight.
  • Diminishing returns inside a category: the strongest signal counts fully, the 2nd at 40%, 3rd at 20%, then 10% and 5%.
  • Combination bonuses: a few pairings that commonly appear together in scams add a small, visible bonus (e.g. a prize that requires a fee).
  • The total is capped at 100 and shown in bands: none, low, review carefully, high caution, very high caution.

The weights are judgement calls made for teaching purposes. They have not been calibrated or validated on a labelled dataset, so the number is an indicator, not a probability.

4. Links

Addresses are parsed with the browser's built-in URL parser and checked for IP-address hosts, “@” tricks, look-alike (punycode) characters, link shorteners, trusted-sounding words placed in subdomains, app-file downloads and similar structural clues. The “real domain” is estimated with a simple rule (last two labels, or three for endings like .co.in); without the full Public Suffix List this can be wrong for unusual endings.

5. Privacy

  • No backend, no analytics, no external fonts, scripts or images.
  • The page's Content-Security-Policy sets connect-src 'none' and img-src data:, so the browser itself refuses any attempt to send data out.
  • Nothing is written to localStorage, cookies or any other storage. Closing the tab discards everything.

6. Getting help in India

  • Money lost or account misused: call 1930 (National Cyber Crime Helpline) as soon as possible, file a complaint at cybercrime.gov.in, and inform your bank through its official helpline.
  • No money lost, but you received a suspected fraud call or message: report it through Chakshu on the Sanchar Saathi portal.

These are the only external links on the page. They open only when you click them.

Known limitations

  • English-language patterns only, plus a handful of romanised Hindi words. Messages in Hindi, Malayalam or other scripts are not understood.
  • Pattern matching has no understanding of meaning: well-written scams can avoid every rule, and genuine messages can trigger some.
  • No knowledge of real organisations, real phone numbers or real websites.
  • Weights are not statistically validated.
